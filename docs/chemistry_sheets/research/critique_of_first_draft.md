# Critique of the first draft (completeness and accuracy check)

**(a) Sentences the cited evidence does not support**
1. **Concrete bullet, "traffic wore away 22 to 74% of tined texture depth [14]".** The 22 to 74% range covers all the test sections. Evidence (14) says the smaller losses came mostly from surfaces with small starting textures, which were usually not tined. Say "22 to 74% of original texture depth across test sections; tined sections tended toward the high end."
2. **Concrete bullet, "asphalt did better once snow and ice were counted [13]".** Read with "California and Wisconsin", this implies both states. The snow-and-ice ratio (1.50 vs 1.26) is from Wisconsin only. It also leaves out that the California transversely tined sections were bridge decks only.
3. **Thin surface treatment bullet, "strongest evidence that heat followed by rain makes roads slick [17][25]".** [25] shows bleeding cuts skid resistance. It says nothing about heat followed by rain. [17] is one interchange case, the same note as [26]. Evidence (31) found that flushing had no significant effect on crash likelihood on Texas asphalt. Say "one case report plus friction findings; a statewide Texas crash study found flushing not significant."
4. **Section 5, "so raw counts understate per-trip risk [40]".** That conclusion comes from [39] and [54]. The authors of [40] give other possible reasons for the high storm crash rate: very low volume and possible over-reporting of crashes in bad weather.
5. **Thin surface treatment bullet, "Many of these are lower-traffic roads [29], and fog crashes cluster on two-lane rural roads [44]".** Putting these side by side implies seal-coat roads are rural two-lane fog-risk roads. No source says that. In (44) the fog weighting is marked as the team's own inference. Either label it as inference or remove it.

**(b) Important mechanisms left out**
1. **Freezing drizzle or mist after a long freeze (33)(34)(36).** Light drizzle or mist after many hours below freezing can leave a thin, nearly invisible glaze, worst on elevated roadway. Speed makes it worse. Ramps and shaded areas freeze first. Drivers staying home lowers traffic. This can be written without naming the event. It fills the "freezing rain evidence is limited" gap, which is most relevant to North Texas.
2. **The one Texas crash study split by surface (31).**
   - Precipitation and daily traffic raised crash likelihood on every pavement type.
   - Skid number mattered in most models.
   - Flushing, rutting and raveling did not.
   - 90% of hydroplaning crashes happened at water films below 0.05 in, thinner than the traditional 0.095 in threshold.
   The sheet uses only the pothole finding.
3. **Worn pavement in rain (22)(12)(30).** Crash rates rise on worn pavements on days with at least 10 mm of rain. Traffic polishing and age wear friction away. On rural Texas curves, wet crash frequency depended on both skid resistance and rainfall. This supports traffic and age as interaction inputs in the road-level model.
4. **First-rain mechanism details (3)(16).** No added fatal risk if it also rained yesterday, which bears directly on claim 1. Drivers are out of practice after a dry spell. Lab work shows dry-spell debris mixes with first rain into a lubricant that later washes off.
5. **Driver adaptation (49)(55).** Speeds fall 10 to 25% on wet arterials, and deeper water was linked to less severe crashes. This explains the "more crashes, fewer deaths" pattern the sheet reports without a reason.
6. **Limits on the heat link (19)(20).** Binder temperature sensitivity is not the main cause of bleeding. Bleeding and flushing concentrate at intersections and places where heavy vehicles brake and turn.
7. **Blow-up conditions (24).** Blow-ups happen at joints or cracks that let in moisture, mainly on jointed concrete, and are more likely after a wet spring followed by heat. The concrete group includes continuously reinforced concrete, so "concrete" overstates the reach.
8. **Smaller items.** Fog is worst on winter mornings and at night without street lights, and speed variance is the stated reason fog raises risk (43)(44). Rain risk peaks in the morning rush (45). On a daily scale, days over 20 mm showed only a 1.47 relative risk (52).

**(c) Dates, years or named events in the main sheet**
None found. Only places and agencies appear (Iowa, Wisconsin, California, Texas, TxDOT). The source list has years, but that is outside the main sheet.

**(d) Overstated certainty**
1. **"Low visibility causes…" and "Severe crosswinds cause…" (Section 6).** The source gives shares of crashes, not causes. Use "accounts for" or "is linked to".
2. **"Lower friction means more wet crashes [11]."** It leaves out the guide's warning that the relationship is site-specific.
3. **Asphalt bullet [7].** It leaves out that the dry-spell scale is months, not days, that it measured friction, not crashes, and that the data are from California asphalt.
4. **Toll roads, "thin water films and hydroplaning matter most [10]".** [10] does not rank road types. Hydroplaning also needs ruts or poor drainage, not just speed.
5. **Wisconsin ratio (Section 2).** It drops "transversely tined" and "rural, under 60,000 vehicles a day". These are minor, but the result does not carry over to DFW urban freeways as written.
