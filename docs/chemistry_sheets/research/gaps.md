# What the researchers looked for and could not find or open

### first_rain
Pages I could not open:
- Full text of Eisenberg (2004). The Berkeley preprint server refused the connection and ScienceDirect returned 403. So I have only the abstract (read from PubMed through NCBI E-utilities), not the regression tables or figures for lags between 2 and 20 days.
- Jaroszweski and McNamara (2014, rainfall and road accidents, Birmingham repository): 403.
- The SWOV records for Eisenberg and for Keay and Simmonds: bot check.
- Full texts of Keay and Simmonds (2006) and Black, Villarini and Mote (2017): paywalled. I read abstracts only.
- Full texts of Rad et al. (2018, Rasht, Iran) and Lobo et al. (2019, Porto) on publisher pages: 403. I read their abstracts through Crossref metadata.

Conflict to note: the Berkeley press release says +3.7% at 2 days and +9.2% at 21 days per cm, while the paper abstract says about 3% at exactly 2 days and about 9% at more than 20 days. Cite the paper's figures.

Opened but left out to stay within 8 claims:
- Brodsky and Hakkert (1988, PubMed): injury-crash risk in rain is 2 to 3 times dry weather, and possibly greater after a dry spell. No numbers for the dry-spell part.
- Eisenberg and Warner (2005, AJPH, PMC1449863): the first snowy day of the year carries a fatality IRR of 1.14 compared with other snow days. The first-snow difference was not significant for injury crashes (1.04) or property-damage-only crashes (1.01).
- Rad et al. (2018, Crossref abstract): the first day after a dry spell had the greatest impact on crashes, and risk rose with spell length. Small single-city study.
- Black, Villarini and Mote (2017, Crossref abstract): crashes +10% and injuries +8% on rain days in six US states from May to September, and +51% crashes above 50 mm. No dry-spell analysis in the abstract.
- Lobo et al. (2019, Crossref abstract): the previous month's rain weakens the rain effect in Porto.

Not found anywhere:
- A study that splits the dry-spell or first-rain crash effect by pavement surface type (concrete vs asphalt vs seal coat).
- Any Texas or DFW-specific study of days since last rain.
- A study that directly measures oil or rubber residue on roads and links it to crash counts. The residue mechanism is offered as a hypothesis in Eisenberg, and as a friction finding in the FHWA and Caltrans pavement work, not as a measured crash cause.
- An FHWA, NHTSA or TxDOT page that states the dry-spell crash effect with numbers.

### wet_friction
I found these but left them out to keep 8 claims. (a) FHWA Technical Advisory T 5040.36, Surface Texture for Asphalt and Concrete Pavements (https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/pavement/t504036.cfm), which I opened. It says both microtexture and macrotexture are needed at low and high speeds, that tining after a burlap drag is a low-cost way to add macrotexture to concrete, and that easily polished aggregates should be avoided. It also cites Swedish research: "for worn pavements, crash rates increase on days with rainfall of 10mm or greater." (b) FHWA-HRT-14-065, ch. 3 (https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/14065/003.cfm), which I opened. It repeats the 0.002 in water film / 20 to 30% friction loss figure and says "The coefficient of friction is most influenced by speed." (c) TTI/TxDOT report 0-7098-R1 (2023, hydroplaning and LiDAR), which I opened. It says storm intensity, drainage basin area, runoff length and slope set water film thickness. It lists rutting, shoulder build-up, bleeding and drainage as wet-crash review items in TxDOT's Wet Weather Accident Reduction Program. It notes IH 20 in Harrison County averaged over 92 wet-surface crashes a year (2015 to 2019). It does not compare concrete with asphalt.

Could not open: the FHWA page 'State Practices to Reduce Wet Weather Skidding Crashes' (HTTP 403); the 2013 UT Austin thesis on skid resistance thresholds for TxDOT (HTTP 403); the SWOV page on rainfall and contaminants (bot check). Searched but not opened: Black et al. 2016 (daily rain over 50 mm linked to +51% crashes), Tamerius et al. 2016 (Iowa hourly radar), the UTSA Texas rainfall and fatal crash paper, and the 2023 TRR paper on RAP in TxDOT surface mixes (friction 0.55 to 0.47). None of these are cited above.

Not found: a peer-reviewed or TxDOT study that gives wet crash rates for concrete vs asphalt-over-concrete vs seal coat on Texas roads; a source giving crash risk by hourly rain intensity split by surface type; a quantified 'first rain after N dry days' friction or crash effect with real road data (the Ifsttar abstract is a lab test only). The FHWA Pavement Friction Management advisory (believed to be T 5040.38) was not located or opened.

### asphalt_heat
1) The TTI 'Get a Grip' page at https://tti.tamu.edu/2013/08/06/get-a-grip now returns HTTP 404 (checked with both WebFetch and curl). I read the Internet Archive copy from Sep 7, 2017. The TTI 2010 water-blasting article is also 404 live and was read from the Archive. 2) Coverage gap: both Texas sources (TTI and TxDOT 0-5230) are about seal coats and surface treatments, the thin surface-treated group (code 10). I found no source showing that thick hot-mix asphalt (code 4) or asphalt over concrete (code 7) flush in DFW summer heat enough to change crash risk. Treat 'heat then rain is worse on asphalt' as best supported for surface-treated roads and weaker for the others. TxDOT 0-5230 also says binder sensitivity to temperature is not the main cause; too much binder, aggregate pressed too deep, and traffic are. 3) I found no source that puts a number on how much crash risk rises when it rains on a flushed or bleeding surface, or after a heat streak, by surface type. Nothing ties a dry or hot spell before rain to extra crashes on asphalt versus concrete. 4) I could not open the FHWA page 'State Practices to Reduce Wet Weather Skidding Crashes' (highways.dot.gov; HTTP 403 to both curl and WebFetch). Its line that wet crashes rise as friction falls is known only from a search snippet and is not used. 5) TRID (trid.trb.org) refused connections, so these FHWA-era seasonal skid reports stayed unopened: 'Predictor Model for Seasonal Variations in Skid Resistance' (TRID 199228), which reportedly used time since last rain and 30-day temperature history; 'Seasonal Skid Resistance Variations' (TRID 71019); and TRID 299548. These would directly support history features, but I did not read them. 6) Not opened: the full 0-5230 technical report and its booklet 'Maintenance Solutions for Bleeding and Flushed Pavements'; FHWA Technical Advisory 5040.38 (Pavement Friction Management); TxDOT 0-6746-01-1 (seal coat skid model); the recent Texas crash-susceptibility study (ROSA P dot/93247). 7) A Wisconsin DOT pavement-temperature threshold of about 114 F for concrete buckling appeared only in a news search snippet and was not opened or used. 8) Copyright: verbatim quotes are given only for the one TTI quote (under 15 words) and for the public-domain federal FHWA pages. For the TxDOT, Nebraska DOT and TTI 2010 pages the quote is withheld and a labelled paraphrase is given instead; all three pages were opened and read.

### ice_cold
Gaps, and what I looked for but could not find or open:

1. **DFW traffic volumes during the February 2021 storm.** I found no TxDOT, NCTCOG or TTI data showing how far traffic fell. The 29% drop and the 7% to 56% range come from Iowa and national snow studies, not North Texas ice.

2. **Total DFW crash counts for the February 2021 storm.** I found no official total. A Dallas Morning News article from Feb 11, 2021, appears in search snippets with Arlington police answering 82 crashes overnight and Grand Prairie reporting 13 major and 54 minor crashes. The page would not load for me, so I did not use those numbers.

3. **Bridges freezing first.** NWS, TxDOT and an FHWA Michigan case study all state it. I found no study giving a crash-rate multiplier for bridges versus at-grade pavement on ice days.

4. **Concrete versus asphalt in freezing weather.** The only evidence is two Utah studies, and they disagree: the 2014 UDOT study found concrete froze first, the 2010 BYU thesis found no average difference. Neither is from Texas. I found nothing on thin seal-coat surfaces in freezing weather.

5. **Sleet and freezing rain separately from snow.** Most crash evidence I found is about snow in northern states. I found no peer-reviewed number for sleet or freezing rain on their own.

6. **Not opened.** I did not open the full NTSB report PDF (HIR-23-01). The NTSB press release (NR20230323A) was opened but not used as a claim. It says the contractor pre-treated the two southbound lanes with brine 44 hours before the crash and did not treat the elevated section that morning.

7. **Opened but not used as claims.** I also opened an Iowa State/Aurora 2005 project summary saying snowy weather "greatly increases crash frequency and crash rate" while severity is slightly lower. I opened the FHWA weather page too: it says 18% of weather-related crashes happen during freezing precipitation, and about 12% of all crashes are weather-related (NHTSA data, 2019 to 2023).

8. **A data limit for this project.** By its own design rules, the project may not use the crash file's own weather or road-surface columns as model inputs, so it cannot pick out ice days from police reports. Freezing days have to come from NOAA's temperature and precipitation records, and there are only 3 to 7 such days a year.

### visibility_wind_storms
Not found or not opened:
(1) The wet-pavement statistic in the topic. The current FHWA impact pages give no separate wet-pavement crash count or percent, only rain/mist figures.
(2) Any peer-reviewed study measuring crash risk from thunderstorms or lightning as such. Thunderstorm downpours are covered only by the heavy-rain figures (Stevens 2019 over 7.6 mm/h, Black 2017 over 50 mm/day).
(3) A relative-risk figure for strong wind on general traffic. Becker et al., 'Weather impacts on various types of road crashes: a quantitative analysis using generalized additive models' (European Transport Research Review 2022, DOI 10.1186/s12544-022-00561-2), was blocked by a Springer login redirect and is not opened. A Wyoming DOT research proposal (Ohara and Young, 2019) was opened but only cites second-hand findings: a Nevada 40 mph gust limit for high-profile vehicles, and overturned trucks averaging 38 mph wind when empty and 51 mph when loaded. These are truck findings only, so I left them out.
(4) A direct wildfire-smoke crash study with effect sizes. The Alberta working paper (Acharya, arts.ucalgary.ca) and the Burton and Roach fatal-crash PM2.5 paper turned up only in search snippets and were not opened. The Sager PM2.5 study is general air pollution, not smoke.
(5) The NOAA Research news page on dust deaths (research.noaa.gov) returned 403, and so did the Princeton page for Black et al. Both papers' abstracts were read on other repository pages instead.
(6) Stevens 2019 gives the South region's relative risk (which covers Texas) only in a figure, not in the text, so no Texas-specific number is reported.
(7) No source tied fog, wind, dust or downpour risk to pavement surface type (concrete, asphalt, seal coat). Surface-specific claims need other sources.
(8) The '5% of fatal crashes in low visibility, 2013-2022' figure from an FHWA Every Day Counts item showed up only in a search snippet. It was not opened and is not used.

### txdot_surface_codes
I could not find any TxDOT CRIS document that decodes surf_type_id codes 7-10, or the new meaning of 5 and 6, for the crash extract itself. The official CRIS Public Extract spec (TxDOT, downloaded and parsed) lists only the old codes 1-6, so the newer meanings are confirmed only through the Roadway Inventory SRF_TYPE spec and PMIS documents. That the crash field copies the inventory field is my inference. I did not open the Standard Extract File Specification, and I did not find the 2024 or 2025 Roadway Inventory Specifications or the GRID data dictionary on txdot.gov. The copy I used is hosted on a UT Austin course page. The TxDOT ArcGIS Roadway Inventory layer has no coded-value domain on SRF_TYPE. The TxDOT PMIS FY2018-2021 annual report (ftp.txdot.gov; WebFetch failed on its certificate, so I fetched it with curl) and the FY2011-2014 report I opened contain no pavement-type code table. I did not check whether PMIS's rule that unrated sections default to type 5 also applies to the Roadway Inventory SRF_TYPE. None of these sources says anything about how weather or crash risk differs by surface; they only define the codes.
