# How weather changes crash risk on different roads

## 1. First rain after a dry spell
Rain raises crashes more after a longer dry spell: each centimeter raised fatal crashes about 3% two days after the last rain and about 9% after more than 20 days, with the same pattern for non-fatal crashes [1]. The effect grows with both spell length and rain amount [5]. Heavy rain yesterday means fewer crashes today, on average, but whether oil washing off or drivers adapting explains this is not proven [2]. Friction is often poor after a long dry spell [8]. No source splits this effect by surface type.

## 2. Wet pavement grip
A water film about 0.05 mm thick, which light rain can make, can cut friction 20 to 30% [10]. It matters little below 20 mph but a lot above 40 mph, and hydroplaning needs deep water plus high speed [10]. Lower friction means more wet crashes [11]. Risk is highest while rain falls and drops back once it stops [6][15]. Rain days raised crashes about 10%, and days over 50 mm about 51% [46]. Rain raises non-fatal crashes much more than fatal ones [4]. On Wisconsin freeways, tined concrete had a wet-to-dry crash ratio of 0.16 against 0.23 for asphalt [13].

## 3. Heat and asphalt
Seasonal swings in skid resistance are larger on asphalt than on concrete, perhaps because dark asphalt absorbs more sun [8][23]. Temperature explained only about 5.7% of skid variation [23]. No source shows thick asphalt or asphalt over concrete flushing enough to change crash risk. On afternoons at 90 F or hotter, concrete slabs can buckle at joints, more often on older concrete, while asphalt overlays push up into bumps [24]. These are sudden local hazards, not lost wet grip [24].

## 4. Thin seal-coat roads
Bleeding can drastically cut seal-coat skid resistance whatever the stone quality [25]. At one busy truck interchange, heat pushed binder up and vehicles slid off when it later rained, a single case rather than a crash-rate study [26]. TxDOT uses seal coats mainly on lower-traffic roads, counting over 10,000 vehicles a day as high traffic [29]. Potholes were tied to crashes only on surface-treated roads [31].

## 5. Ice and cold
Bridges and overpasses freeze first, and black ice can form after rain if wet roads drop below freezing overnight [35]. Snow days had 7% fewer fatal crashes but 23% more injury crashes and 45% more property-damage crashes than dry days, and the first snowy day of the year was deadlier than later ones [39]. Winter storms cut Iowa interstate traffic about 29%, so raw counts understate per-trip risk [40]. Evidence on concrete versus asphalt in freezing weather is mixed [37][38]. Evidence on sleet or freezing rain alone is limited.

## 6. Fog, storms, wind and haze
Low visibility causes about 4% of weather-related crashes but 13% of weather-related deaths [42]. Fog crashes cluster on high-speed, undivided and two-lane rural roads [44]. No source studies thunderstorms directly, but heavy precipitation raised fatal-crash risk about 2.5 times, and daily totals hide short downpours [45]. Severe crosswinds cause about 0.1% of crashes [41]. Evidence on haze is weak [48].

## 7. Exposure and holidays
Light rain barely changes traffic, while heavy rain cuts flow 10 to 20% [50]. So rain-day crash counts mostly reflect higher risk per vehicle [51]. Holiday evidence covers deaths only and does not show whether total crashes rise or fall [56].

## What this means for each road type
- **Concrete:** In California and Wisconsin freeway data, tined concrete had wet crash ratios as good as or better than asphalt, but asphalt did better once snow and ice were counted [13]. In Texas, traffic wore away 22 to 74% of tined texture depth [14]. Very hot afternoons bring a risk of blow-ups [24].
- **Asphalt over concrete:** No crash study tests this surface directly. It can push up into bumps in heat [24].
- **Asphalt:** Seasonal friction swings are larger than on concrete [8][23]. Wet skid resistance fell with longer dry spells, higher heat and more traffic [7]. Evidence that heat followed by rain adds crashes on this surface is missing.
- **Thin surface treatment:** This surface has the strongest evidence that heat followed by rain makes roads slick [17][25]. Many of these are lower-traffic roads [29], and fog crashes cluster on two-lane rural roads [44].
- **Toll roads (surface unknown):** Where they run at freeway speed, thin water films and hydroplaning matter most [10]. Any bridges or elevated sections freeze first [35].
- **City streets (surface unknown):** At low speed, fine surface texture provides most of the grip [9]. Urban counties saw the largest relative rise in crashes on rain days [46].
