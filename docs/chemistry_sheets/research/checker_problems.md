# Problems the three checkers found in the revised draft (all were then fixed or judged wrong by the fix agent)

1. **[citations]** "Our sources do not split this by surface type."
   - Problem: Factual claim with no citation, and only partly true. Evidence [7] is asphalt-only: it found that wet skid resistance falls as dry periods get longer. Evidence [8] says seasonal friction swings are larger on asphalt than on concrete. What the sources lack is a crash study split by surface, not surface-specific evidence of any kind.
   - Fix suggested: None of our crash studies split this by surface type; the only surface-specific evidence is friction data, where California asphalt lost wet skid resistance over dry periods of months [7].

2. **[citations]** "In two Canadian cities, risk was highest while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped [6], and fatal risk peaks in the morning rush [45]."
   - Problem: The second clause misstates [45] and seems to move it to Canada. Evidence 45 is a US-wide radar study. What peaks in the morning rush is the added risk from precipitation (relative risk about 1.0 overnight up to about 1.6), not fatal crash risk overall. The first clause also drops the size of the effect: about 70% above normal during rain.
   - Fix suggested: In two Canadian cities, risk was about 70% above normal while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped [6]. Across the US, the added fatal risk from precipitation peaked in the morning rush, at a relative risk of about 1.6 [45].

3. **[citations]** "Binder heat sensitivity is not the main cause of bleeding [19], which concentrates where heavy vehicles brake and turn [20]."
   - Problem: Overstated, and the scope is missing. Evidence 20 says only that intersections 'can be especially problematic' when heavy vehicles turn and brake; it does not say bleeding concentrates there. Both [19] and [20] are about seal coats and surface treatments, not asphalt in general.
   - Fix suggested: On seal coats and surface treatments, binder heat sensitivity is not the main cause of bleeding [19], and intersections where heavy vehicles brake and turn can be especially problematic [20].

4. **[citations]** "Concrete can instead blow up at joints or cracks that let in moisture, mainly jointed concrete, usually on afternoons of 90 F or hotter and more often when old or after a wet spring, a sudden hazard rather than lost wet grip [24]."
   - Problem: Place and conditions are missing. The source is Nebraska DOT, not Texas. Its condition is a wet spring followed by very hot weather, not a wet spring alone. As written, the sentence reads like a general or Texas finding.
   - Fix suggested: Nebraska DOT says concrete, mainly jointed concrete, can instead blow up at joints or cracks that let in moisture, usually on afternoons of 90 F or hotter, and more often in older concrete and after a wet spring followed by heat. This is a sudden hazard, not lost wet grip [24].

5. **[citations]** "Concrete-versus-asphalt freezing evidence is mixed and from Utah [37][38]."
   - Problem: Only [37] is placed in Utah (US 40 near Heber). Evidence 38 is a BYU thesis whose study site is not stated. The sentence also leaves out that each study warns its results depend on the site.
   - Fix suggested: Concrete-versus-asphalt freezing evidence is mixed and site-specific: one Utah mountain site study and one BYU thesis [37][38].

6. **[citations]** "In Florida, fog and smoke crashes were most common on winter mornings, at night without street lights, and on high-speed, undivided and rural two-lane roads [44]."
   - Problem: Overstated. Evidence 44 supports 'most common' only for winter mornings. For high-speed, undivided and rural two-lane roads and for unlit nights, it says these crashes were 'more common' or 'more likely' than clear-visibility crashes, not most common.
   - Fix suggested: In Florida, fog and smoke crashes were most common on winter mornings and, compared with clear-visibility crashes, more likely at night without street lights and on high-speed, undivided and rural two-lane roads [44].

7. **[citations]** "So rain-day counts mostly reflect higher per-vehicle risk [51]."
   - Problem: Presented as a sourced finding, but it is the evidence writer's inference. The source measured traffic at one count site in Melbourne and found wet days cut daily volume by only about 1.4% in winter and 2.1% in spring. It does not draw the per-vehicle-risk conclusion.
   - Fix suggested: At one Melbourne count site, wet days cut daily traffic by only about 1 to 2% [51], so rain-day counts likely reflect mostly higher per-vehicle risk; that last step is our inference.

8. **[citations]** "On rural Wisconsin freeways under 60,000 vehicles a day, transversely tined concrete had a wet-to-dry crash ratio of 0.16 against 0.23 for asphalt, but asphalt did better with snow and ice [13]."
   - Problem: Leaves out the same study's result that fits DFW interstates better. On California urban freeways over 60,000 vehicles a day, transversely tined concrete and asphalt had the same wet-to-dry ratio, about 0.20, and the tined sections there were bridge decks only. Citing only the Wisconsin figure makes the concrete advantage look stronger than the source shows.
   - Fix suggested: On rural Wisconsin freeways under 60,000 vehicles a day, transversely tined concrete had a wet-to-dry crash ratio of 0.16 against 0.23 for asphalt, but on California urban freeways over 60,000 a day the two were equal at about 0.20, and asphalt did better once snow and ice were included [13].

9. **[citations]** "Evidence is one case report plus friction findings, and statewide, flushing was not significant [17][25][31]."
   - Problem: Contradicted by [31] itself. That statewide Texas study built a crash model for surface-treated asphalt, and potholes were linked to crashes only on that surface. So there is crash evidence beyond one case report and friction data.
   - Fix suggested: Evidence is one case report, friction findings and one statewide Texas crash model, in which potholes were linked to crashes on surface-treated roads but flushing was not significant [17][25][31].

10. **[certainty]** "On rural Wisconsin freeways under 60,000 vehicles a day, transversely tined concrete had a wet-to-dry crash ratio of 0.16 against 0.23 for asphalt, but asphalt did better with snow and ice [13]."
   - Problem: This applies a rural, low-traffic result to concrete in general and leaves out the same study's urban result, which is the closer match to DFW's busy concrete freeways. On California urban freeways over 60,000 vehicles a day, the wet-to-dry ratio was about 0.20 for both transversely tined concrete and asphalt, with no advantage for tined concrete (evidence 13). Neither result is Texas data.
   - Fix suggested: On California urban freeways over 60,000 vehicles a day, the wet-to-dry crash ratio was about 0.20 for both transversely tined concrete and asphalt (0.16 for longitudinally tined). Only on rural Wisconsin freeways under 60,000 a day did tined concrete do better (0.16 vs 0.23), and asphalt did better once snow and ice were counted [13]. None of this is Texas data.

11. **[certainty]** "In two Canadian cities, risk was highest while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped [6], and fatal risk peaks in the morning rush [45]."
   - Problem: Source 45 says the extra risk from precipitation (the relative risk) peaks at about 1.6 in the morning rush. It does not say that fatal crash risk overall peaks then. Joining the two clauses with 'and' also makes it read as if the Canadian study found it.
   - Fix suggested: In two Canadian cities, risk was about 70% above normal while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped, even on roads that were still wet [6]. Across the US, the added fatal risk from precipitation was largest in the morning rush (relative risk about 1.6, against about 1.0 overnight) [45].

12. **[certainty]** "Hydroplaning needs deep water plus speed [10], yet in a statewide Texas study 90% of hydroplaning crashes came at estimated films under 0.05 in [31]."
   - Problem: 'Yet' sets up a contradiction that isn't really there: 0.05 in is about 1.3 mm, so it is not a thin film. The sentence also leaves out that a language model picked out the 627 hydroplaning crashes from crash narratives, and that the water depths are model averages. Coming right after the 0.05 mm figure, the 0.05 in figure invites a mix-up of units.
   - Fix suggested: Hydroplaning needs deep water plus speed [10]. A statewide Texas study used a language model to find 627 hydroplaning crashes in crash narratives and put 90% of them at modelled water films under 0.05 in (about 1.3 mm), below the traditional 0.095 in threshold [31].

13. **[certainty]** "Binder heat sensitivity is not the main cause of bleeding [19], which concentrates where heavy vehicles brake and turn [20]."
   - Problem: Both findings come from a TxDOT study of seal coats and surface treatments, but under a 'Heat and asphalt' heading they read as true of all asphalt. The source also says only that intersections 'can be especially problematic', not that bleeding concentrates there.
   - Fix suggested: On TxDOT seal coats, binder heat sensitivity was judged not to be the main cause of bleeding [19], and intersections where heavy vehicles brake and turn can be especially problematic [20].

14. **[certainty]** "Evidence is one case report plus friction findings, and statewide, flushing was not significant [17][25][31]."
   - Problem: This contradicts section 4, which cites crash evidence for surface-treated roads from the same statewide models [31]: potholes were linked to crashes only on surface-treated roads. Saying the evidence is only one case report plus friction findings understates what the sources contain.
   - Fix suggested: Heat-then-rain evidence is one case report plus friction findings. Statewide crash models found potholes, but not flushing, linked to crashes on surface-treated roads [17][25][31].

15. **[certainty]** "Urban counties saw the largest relative rain-day rise [46]."
   - Problem: This is a county-level result covering all roads in a county, freeways included, but it is placed under 'City streets' as if it were about city streets. It also comes from six states, none of them Texas.
   - Fix suggested: Across six states, urban counties (all their roads, not only city streets) saw the largest relative rain-day rise [46].

16. **[certainty]** "So rain-day counts mostly reflect higher per-vehicle risk [51]."
   - Problem: This generalizes from one urban count site in Melbourne, and only holds for light-to-moderate rain. The sentence just before it says heavy rain cuts traffic 10 to 20%, so on heavy-rain days raw counts understate per-vehicle risk.
   - Fix suggested: So on light-to-moderate rain days, counts mostly reflect higher per-vehicle risk (one Melbourne count site lost only 1 to 3% of traffic) [51]. On heavy-rain days, lower traffic means raw counts understate it [50].

17. **[certainty]** "Concrete can instead blow up at joints or cracks that let in moisture, mainly jointed concrete, usually on afternoons of 90 F or hotter and more often when old or after a wet spring, a sudden hazard rather than lost wet grip [24]."
   - Problem: The 90 F timing and the wet-spring pattern come from one Nebraska DOT safety page but are stated as general fact. 90 F afternoons are routine in a DFW summer, so a Nebraska threshold may not carry over.
   - Fix suggested: Nebraska DOT says concrete can instead blow up at joints or cracks that let in moisture, mainly jointed concrete, usually on afternoons of 90 F or hotter and more often when old or after a wet spring. It is a sudden hazard rather than lost wet grip, and the timing comes from Nebraska, not Texas [24].

18. **[certainty]** "Bridges, overpasses, ramps and shaded areas freeze first [35][36]."
   - Problem: TxDOT says these places 'tend to freeze first', and the NWS names only bridges and overpasses. The sheet states it as always true.
   - Fix suggested: Bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces, and TxDOT adds that ramps and shaded areas tend to freeze first too [35][36].

19. **[certainty]** "Federal investigators found ice on an elevated Fort Worth freeway, after 36 hours below freezing, made drivers lose control [33]."
   - Problem: The NTSB also named drivers going too fast for the conditions (on a 75 mph road) as a contributing factor. Leaving that out makes ice look like the only cause.
   - Fix suggested: Federal investigators found ice on an elevated Fort Worth freeway, after 36 hours below freezing, made drivers lose control, with speed too high for the conditions a contributing factor [33].

20. **[leakage_clarity]** "Federal investigators found ice on an elevated Fort Worth freeway, after 36 hours below freezing, made drivers lose control [33]."
   - Problem: Leakage. This is the NTSB finding on the I-35W Fort Worth pileup (130 vehicles, six deaths) of one specific 2021 morning. It tells the decision model about an actual DFW crash outcome on an identifiable day, and naming the elevated Fort Worth freeway points straight at a road in the study area. The critique also says this mechanism can be written without naming the event.
   - Fix suggested: Federal investigators found that ice building up on an elevated freeway after 36 hours below freezing made drivers lose control, with speed too high for the conditions a contributing factor [33].

21. **[leakage_clarity]** "In North Texas, light freezing drizzle days into a long freeze left a nearly invisible glaze on many roads [34]."
   - Problem: Leakage. The source is the NWS Fort Worth write-up of one storm inside the data years, so 'In North Texas' turns a general mechanism into a report of road conditions in the study area on identifiable days. That source also describes crashes and the pileup on those days. As worded, the sentence is also hard to parse: 'drizzle days into a long freeze' reads as a typo.
   - Fix suggested: Light freezing drizzle that falls several days into a long freeze can leave a nearly invisible glaze on many roads [34].

22. **[leakage_clarity]** "In two Canadian cities, risk was highest while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped [6], and fatal risk peaks in the morning rush [45]."
   - Problem: Misreading risk. Joined to the Canadian sentence, the second clause reads as a Canadian finding, and it says that fatal crash risk overall peaks in the morning rush. Source [45] is a US radar study, and what peaks in the morning rush (about 1.6 times, against about 1.0 overnight) is the extra fatal risk while precipitation is falling.
   - Fix suggested: In two Canadian cities, risk was highest while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped [6]. US radar data show the added fatal risk while precipitation falls peaks in the morning rush [45].

23. **[leakage_clarity]** "Hydroplaning needs deep water plus speed [10], yet in a statewide Texas study 90% of hydroplaning crashes came at estimated films under 0.05 in [31]."
   - Problem: Misreading risk from units. The sentence just before it uses 0.05 mm, and this one uses 0.05 in. A reader can easily take them as the same film, but 0.05 in is about 25 times thicker. The sentence also leaves out the threshold that makes the finding mean something.
   - Fix suggested: Hydroplaning needs deep water plus speed [10], yet in a statewide Texas study 90% of hydroplaning crashes came at estimated films under 0.05 in (about 1.3 mm), below the traditional 0.095 in threshold [31].
