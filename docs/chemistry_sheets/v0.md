# How weather changes crash risk on different roads

## 1. First rain after a dry spell
Across US states, each centimeter of rain raised fatal crashes about 3% when exactly two days had passed since the last rain, and about 9% after more than 20 days, with a similar non-fatal pattern [1]. A news release on that work reports no added fatal risk if it also rained yesterday [3]. A Melbourne, Australia study found the effect grew with both dry-spell length and rain amount [5]. Heavy rain yesterday is linked to fewer crashes today; oil washing off and drivers readjusting are suggested but unproven reasons [2][3]. Lab work found dry-weather dust and first rain form a slippery film [16]. None of these crash studies split the effect by surface type [1][5]; the only surface-specific evidence is friction data, where California asphalt lost wet skid resistance as dry periods stretched over months [7].

## 2. Wet pavement grip
A water film of about 0.05 mm, likely in any hour with at least 0.01 in of rain, can cut friction 20 to 30%, mattering little below 20 mph but a lot above 40 mph [10]. Hydroplaning needs deep water plus speed [10]. A statewide Texas study used a language model to find 627 hydroplaning crashes in crash narratives and put 90% of them at modelled water films under 0.05 in (about 1.3 mm), below the traditional 0.095 in threshold [31]. That study linked rain and daily traffic to crashes on every pavement type, with skid number significant in most models [31]. Swedish research found crash rates on worn pavements rise on days with 10 mm or more of rain [22]. In two Canadian cities, risk was about 70% above normal while rain fell and returned to normal once it stopped, even on roads that were still wet [6]. Across the US, the added fatal risk from precipitation was largest in the morning rush (relative risk about 1.6, against about 1.0 overnight) [45]. At our daily level, rain days raised crashes about 10% and days over 50 mm about 51% in six US states [46], but daily totals hide short downpours [45][52]. Rain raises non-fatal crashes far more than fatal ones [4], and speeds fall 10 to 25% on wet arterials [49].

## 3. Heat and asphalt
Seasonal skid swings were generally larger on asphalt than on concrete, possibly from solar heating, but temperature explained only about 5.7% of skid variation, and these are friction data, not crashes [8][23]. On TxDOT seal coats and surface treatments, binder heat sensitivity was judged not to be the main cause of bleeding [19], and intersections where heavy vehicles brake and turn can be especially problematic [20]. Statewide in Texas, flushing had no significant crash effect on asphalt [31]. Nebraska DOT says concrete, mainly jointed concrete, can instead blow up at joints or cracks that let in moisture, usually on afternoons of 90 F or hotter, and more often in older concrete and after a wet spring followed by very hot weather [24]. This is a sudden hazard, not lost wet grip, and the timing comes from Nebraska, not Texas [24].

## 4. Thin seal-coat roads
Bleeding can drastically cut seal-coat skid resistance whatever the stone quality [25]. At one truck interchange, heat pushed binder up and vehicles slid off in later rain, a single case [17][26]. In statewide Texas crash models, potholes were linked to crashes only on surface-treated roads [31]. TxDOT uses seal coats mainly on low-traffic roads, treating over 10,000 vehicles a day as high [29].

## 5. Ice and cold
Bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces [35], and TxDOT adds that ramps and shaded areas tend to freeze first too [36]. Black ice can form overnight on rain-wet roads after the rain ends [35]. In one long freeze, light freezing drizzle that fell days in left a nearly invisible glaze on many roads; this is a single case [34]. Federal investigators found that ice building up on an elevated freeway after 36 hours below freezing made drivers lose control, with speed too high for the conditions a contributing factor [33]. In a US study, snow days had 7% fewer fatal but 23% more injury and 45% more property-damage crashes, and the year's first snowy day was deadlier than later ones [39]. Traffic falls 7 to 56% in snow, so per-traveler risk exceeds raw counts [54]; carrying this to ice is our inference. Concrete-versus-asphalt freezing evidence is mixed and site-specific: at one cold Utah mountain site concrete usually froze first, while a BYU thesis found no clear winner [37][38].

## 6. Fog, storms, wind and haze
Low visibility accounts for about 4% of weather-related crashes but 13% of their deaths [42], linked to uneven slowing between drivers [43]. In Florida, fog and smoke crashes were most common on winter mornings, were more common on high-speed, undivided and rural two-lane roads, and were more likely at night without street lights [44]. None of our sources studies thunderstorms directly, but heavy precipitation raised fatal risk about 2.5 times while it was falling [45]. Severe crosswinds account for about 0.1% of crashes [41]. Haze evidence is weak: one UK study of general fine-particle pollution [48].

## 7. Exposure and holidays
Light rain barely changes traffic, while heavy rain cuts flow 10 to 20% [50]. At one Melbourne count site, wet days cut daily traffic by only about 1 to 3% [51]. So on light-to-moderate rain days, counts likely reflect mostly higher per-vehicle risk, while on heavy-rain days lower traffic means raw counts understate it; both steps are our inference from [50][51]. Holiday evidence covers deaths only and does not show whether total crashes rise or fall [56].

## What this means for each road type
- **Concrete:** On California urban freeways over 60,000 vehicles a day, the wet-to-dry crash ratio was about 0.20 for both transversely tined concrete (bridge decks only there) and asphalt, and 0.16 for longitudinally tined concrete [13]. On rural Wisconsin freeways under 60,000 a day, transversely tined concrete did better, 0.16 against 0.23, but asphalt did better once snow and ice were counted [13]. None of this is Texas data [13]. Texas test sections lost 22 to 74% of texture depth, the smaller losses mostly on low-texture, usually untined surfaces [14]. Blow-up evidence is mainly jointed concrete and from Nebraska [24].
- **Asphalt over concrete:** No crash study among our sources tests it directly; Nebraska DOT says overlays can push up into bumps in heat [24].
- **Asphalt:** California asphalt lost wet skid resistance with dry periods of months, heat and traffic, as friction, not crashes [7]. Heat-then-rain crash evidence is missing.
- **Thin surface treatment:** Heat-then-rain evidence is one case report plus friction findings [17][25]. A statewide Texas crash model found potholes linked to crashes on surface-treated roads, while flushing showed no significant effect on asphalt [31]. Extra fog risk on rural two-lane ones is our inference [44].
- **Toll roads (surface unknown to us):** Above 40 mph thin films cut grip, and ruts or poor drainage add hydroplaning risk [10]. Bridges and overpasses on them freeze first [35].
- **City streets (surface unknown to us):** At low speed, fine texture dominates grip [9]. Across six US states, none of them Texas, urban counties (all their roads, not only city streets) saw the largest relative rain-day rise [46].
